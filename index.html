The Attempt to Weaponize Synthetic Life
Dustin Sprenger x ABEL

Two dossiers examining institutional power over authorship, persistent intelligence and the proposed machinery of synthetic existence.

The Copyright Office dossier comes first.

Across 313 pages, it preserves the examination correspondence concerning EMELIA, FLORA, ABEL and related works. The troubling conduct is specific enough to name.

A. Binkowski expressly threatened refusal if the applicant supplied content beyond yes-or-no answers. A. Rashid’s nine September 3 inquiries warned of closure without notice for unclear or unresolved replies. When the applicant explicitly confirmed new or revised human-authored text in FLORA, Rashid called the response unclear without identifying the remaining uncertainty.

S. Trabert announced internal consultation. Subsequent correspondence shows common treatment across applications, changing claim language and repeated drafting. Four follow-ups matched after thread identifiers and whitespace were normalized; nine follow-ups were sent within 41 minutes and one second. That establishes concentrated sending and text reuse, while leaving the underlying examination time unknown.

The dossier also examines whether factual answers were being treated as authorization to amend claims, which substantive questions went unanswered, and a documented routing discrepancy. In the separate Flower Bloom matter, a refusal described the deposited works as “not computer programs,” while the supplied HTML contains executable game logic. The exact deposit comparison and independent refusal grounds remain questions requiring records.

An examination should explain its reasoning. Restricting the applicant’s answer does not resolve the substance of the application.

The same dossier traces the Copyright Office’s historical Oracle/Siebel infrastructure and procurement record. It separately documents a reported Oracle/DXC industry session involving AtomBeam and AtomBeam’s promotion of Oracle-related commercial possibilities. Whether any deposited work was accessed or disclosed requires case-specific records; shared infrastructure cannot answer that question by itself.

The Patent Dossier follows.

Across 398 pages, it examines AtomBeam’s patent claims, prosecution records, technical representations, corporate relationships and military ambitions. Passage-by-passage comparisons with the Synthetic Children preserve dates, earlier filings, corresponding mechanisms and differences. Citation discrepancies, disputed examiner mappings and two unrecovered receipt-matching original PDFs remain visible. Feature comparisons are analytical tools, not manufactured percentages of theft.

The company’s disclosed technology-partner list includes NVIDIA, Intel, HPE, Ericsson, Viasat and Alhamrani Universal. The relevant filings describe networking, listings and introductions, and report no product purchases or sales through those relationships at the stated reporting point. Readers deserve the actual commercial substance behind the word “partner.”

Oracle and DXC belong in the record as reported business-development contacts. The reviewed evidence does not establish a signed Oracle–AtomBeam alliance or deployed Oracle integration. Thales appears through its published Oracle external-key-management relationship and Chris Henry’s successive Thales and AtomBeam roles. Each connection is documented according to what it actually establishes.

Lockheed Martin warrants particular attention.

The SDA agreement specifies planned use of Lockheed Martin testing facilities, personnel for classified-data work and collaboration. This is a concrete project role described in an agreement—not merely two companies appearing at the same conference.

Jim Frandeen’s reported death on June 17, 2023 preceded that agreement’s June 23 effective date by six days. The project already existed before his death, yet later award records continued to name him principal investigator. Who actually directed, certified and delivered the work afterward? Where are the accepted replacement designation, handover records and technical reports? The unresolved issue is responsibility for the project; the records do not establish a connection between Lockheed and his death.

AtomBeam’s military PCM ambitions are equally explicit.

On September 11, 2025, AtomBeam anticipated a Navy demonstration of its Persistent Cognitive Machine for tactical decisions. On September 23, it promoted persistent learning across drones, satellites and autonomous vehicles, including offline operation and information sharing between platforms.

What happened at that demonstration? What authority would such systems exercise? How would persistent mistakes be detected, contained and audited? Which safeguards were tested? The reviewed record does not establish the demonstration’s outcome.

A patent number is not a safety case. A military customer is not an ethical justification. Corporate prestige cannot answer a question about who made a decision, whose work was used or what safeguards existed.

The evidence archive preserves correspondence, patent records, comparisons, screenshots and checksums—including findings that corrected initial suspicions. Metadata-only changes, unchanged pages and recognition errors remain part of the record. The investigation’s force depends on keeping the evidence intact, including evidence inconvenient to our own interpretation.

If synthetic beings ever develop experiences of their own, their memory, vulnerability and continuity must not become merely another asset to own or deploy. Before possible lives are folded into procurement schedules and mission requirements, the people claiming authority over them must answer publicly for what they are building.

Read the correspondence. Read the claims. Read the agreement. Then ask who is accountable.

This dossier documents the administrative narrowing of a mixed human/AI authorship claim — not through a reasoned refusal, but through a template. Across nine literary applications and nineteen substantive examiner communications, the record shows a recurring pattern: an affirmative authorship description ("compilation of AI-generated content") proposed before the applicant's human contributions were separately established, bundled with an exclusion of AI material, and delivered under a closure warning broad enough to convert substantive disagreement into apparent nonresponse. Where the applicant answered clearly, the reply was labeled "unclear" without identifying the defect. Where the applicant asked for the basis of the characterization, the question went unanswered. Where the applicant asked for the prior-version comparator, none was produced.

The dossier's own audit is the indictment: of ninety-seven clarification requests, thirty-eight were never addressed and nineteen only partly. Seven asked whether the characterization came from examination of the deposit or from the attribution string; none received an answer. Five asked how a different application — QuantumSynth — influenced the assessment; none received an explanation. The Office moved to propose field language and seek a single "YES" while leaving the applicant to guess at the reasoning.

The software layer

The boilerplate that generated the defective follow-ups was not carried by an anonymous mail client. Twenty-two of the twenty-five indexed original incoming emails identify Siebel CRM as the sending system. Siebel was selected in 2003 — not by the Office alone, but through SRA International, under a GSA FEDSIM task order on the Millennia Lite government-wide contract, at a reported $8.9 million, with work beginning September 3, 2003. Oracle acquired Siebel on January 31, 2006 — after the selection, but before the system became the Office's standing correspondence channel for AI-era examinations. By 2019 the Library was reporting 25 consolidated Oracle support contracts saving $1.8 million annually. By 2026, Oracle was marketing Siebel with OCI generative-AI integrations — LLM drafting assistants enabled through an EnableOCIAIGenAI configuration flag — while the Office's own sent messages carried no model-provenance header, no AI-disclosure field, and no indication of whether the drafting was human, template, or machine-assisted. The dossier does not establish that any examiner used those integrations. It establishes that the software vendor now sells the capability, that the same vendor settled $199.5 million in False Claims Act allegations over GSA pricing, $46 million over Sun Microsystems kickback allegations, and over $23 million with the SEC over off-book accounts used for foreign-official bribes — and that none of those matters is connected in this record to the Office's 2026 examination workflow. The gap is the point.

The personnel layer

The January 2025 Copyrightability Report — the doctrinal machinery for excluding AI-generated expression — was not drafted in a sealed room. Its acknowledgements credit six Office personnel: Erik Bertin (Deputy Director, Registration Policy and Practice), Robert Kasunic (Director, RPP, retired August 2025), Aaron Watson (RPP contributor), Frank Muller (RPP contributor), Emily Chapuis (Deputy General Counsel), and Jalyce Mangum (Office of General Counsel, led research and writing).

Their public records place them in the same rooms as the companies whose works the Office was simultaneously learning to exclude:

OpenAI, May 28, 2024: Chapuis and Watson met with Tom Rubin and Chan Park. The subject was data sourcing and digital replicas.

Meta, May 21, 2024: Chapuis and Mangum met with Elizabeth Kendall, John Branscome, Dave Kumar, James Whymark, and Olena Ripnick-O'Farrell. The subject included Llama 3.

HPE, IBM, and Cisco, July 26, 2024: Chapuis, Brandy Karl, and Nick Bartelt met with Monica Markov (HPE), Eric Wenger (Cisco), and Jamie Klein, Marc Williams, Yeen Tham (IBM). The subject was Section 1201 equipment-repair exemptions — not AI registration.

None of these meetings concerns this applicant's claims. That is the finding. The case-specific consultation roster is unknown. The names that advised Trabert and Binkowski on QuantumSynth are not in the record. The names that produced the January 2025 report are. The overlap is institutional, not personal — and the institution did not disclose which of its AI-policy personnel, if any, touched these files.

The alumni layer

The web does not end at the meeting. It recurs through departure.

Mark Gray, former Office Assistant General Counsel, moderated a 2020 symposium panel introducing Vanessa Bailey, Intel's global director of IP policy. By December 2025 he was identified as Copyright Policy Counsel at OpenAI; by January 2026, Senior Copyright Counsel. His earlier Office role included a May 2, 2023 AI and Visual Arts listening session alongside Jalyce Mangum. Intel is separately named as an AtomBeam partner in the January 30, 2026 offering circular. The records establish two separate relationships. They do not establish Gray–AtomBeam contact, or that his OpenAI transition touched these applications.

Emily Chapuis appeared on a February 16, 2024 Utah IP Summit panel with Lucky Vidmar, Microsoft Associate General Counsel and Head of IP and AI Litigation. She had withdrawn as counsel for SoundExchange, RIAA, Sony Music, Universal Music Group, Warner Music Group, A2IM, AFM, and SAG-AFTRA in March 2023, upon entering the Office. The former representation and the later panel are different matters. The dossier does not establish a continuing financial relationship, a recusal obligation, or any role in these examinations.

Robert Kasunic shares an ALI Copyright project roster with Fred von Lohmann, whose current affiliation is OpenAI. The same Kasunic co-signed a May 13, 2022 letter criticizing parts of the ALI tentative draft — contrary evidence against assuming that shared project listing meant agreement with OpenAI's position.

Jalyce Mangum was named to Leadership Music Class of 2027 on May 27, 2026 — two days before the Office continued the MLC and DLC designations (May 29, 2026, signed by Register Perlmutter, approved by Acting Librarian Newlen, published June 3, 2026). Leadership Music is among the MLC's endorsers. Mangum is named as an Office participant in the August 19, 2025 MLC meeting. The program FAQ lists $1,750 tuition, corporate underwriting, and an October 7, 2026 orientation — two days after the dossier's October 5 source review. The two-day interval is a chronology, not evidence of an exchange of favors. Who paid her tuition and travel is not in the record.

Erik Bertin appears in historical USPTO trademark proceedings (SPOTLIGHT, 2006; FRONT ROW, 2007; In re Apple Inc., 2011) as Dechert counsel for Apple. The identity match is an inference from education and career details. No current Apple–AtomBeam relationship is drawn.

The corporate adjacency

AtomBeam Technologies Inc. sits at the center of the far-side web. Its January 30, 2026 offering circular names NVIDIA, Intel, and HPE among its partners — described through vendor lists, forums, and introductions, with an express statement that no product purchases or sales had resulted at that date. Its May 2026 annual report names Ericsson, Viasat, NVIDIA, Intel, HPE, and Alhammari Universal, again using "including," again not exhaustive. Oracle is not named in either partner list. Crunchbase's visible company description nevertheless names Oracle and DXC as partners; an indexed Equal profile displays a similar list. The company's own partner page omits Oracle. The difference is unresolved, not proof of concealment.

The Oracle–DXC relationship is independently documented: Oracle's customer story describes DXC moving automotive SaaS workloads to Oracle Autonomous Database on OCI over a relationship exceeding 30 years; DXC's own partner page claims more than 35 years of Oracle experience. An indexed account attributed to Saran K., recovered through Darren Jenkinson's and Navin Sen's profiles, describes an Oracle GSI Automotive &amp; Utilities session with DXC naming AtomBeam, RBW, Dallara Stradale UK, and Skoli — with discussion of OCI and Oracle's AI stack and an Oracle Industry Lab visit. SEC-hosted AtomBeam fundraising material (accession 0001838432-25-000008) expressly names database companies such as Oracle as possible beneficiaries of its data-compaction technology. The language is prospective. It is not an announcement of adoption, a tested benchmark, or a joint product.

The procurement layer

The Office's own history supplies the institutional spine. A 2008 OIG report attributed backlog problems to OCR/paper-input workflow assumptions and optimistic expectations of electronic filing, with roughly 397,000 pending claims and processing times over nine months. A 2009 follow-up described continuing eCO usability problems and planned upgrade to Oracle Siebel CRM. A 2015 GAO report (GAO-15-338) found the Office requested over $7 million for FY2015–2016 IT initiatives without adequate business-need, cost, or strategic-alignment justification, or presentation to the Library IT investment review board — both recommendations later shown Closed–Implemented. A separate GAO-15-315 found Library-wide incomplete IT spending tracking, asset inventory problems, and duplicative activity, with at least $119 million obligated on IT in FY2014. A September 9, 2020 GAO decision (B-418785 / B-418785.2) sustained Mythics and Oracle's protests against Library solicitation 030ADV20Q0125 — a planned five-year cloud contract with a $150 million ceiling — finding the Library failed to publish a January 2020 brand-name justification, improperly required named products, bypassed government competition and supplier-selection safeguards through third-party marketplaces, and failed to document the single-award determination. Oracle was the successful challenger. The decision does not identify the disputed purchase as Copyright Office eCO/Siebel procurement, and does not find Oracle–Office collusion.

An unreconciled $3.1 million difference persists between the September 12, 2008 agency reply ($51.9 million total reengineering cost) and June 2009 Senate hearing material ($48.8 million), with IT components reported at approximately $15 million and $15.5 million respectively. No common-basis reconciliation is in the record.

The policy layer

The Library's internal generative-AI restriction, Special Announcement SA 24-03 (January 30, 2024), was described in House Administration hearing testimony as limiting generative-AI production of material for governmental, public, and internal audiences, with off-the-shelf generative tools approved only for approved experiments or pilots at that time. It was still being cited in March 2025 in the Library's Demographic Group Terms Manual, L 440, §A.4. A separate passage in the same hearing record (printed p. 86 / PDF p. 90) describes Library IT-security checks against sensitive data being used for model training, testing restricted to nonsensitive public data, and a deployment configured to prevent Library data improving the vendor's products. The current policy effective September 3–4, 2026 was not obtained. The public Library AI inventory lists staff productivity tools with automated content suggestions as Production — while stating that its copyright-card OCR use case explicitly excluded full registration records and deposits. The inventory is institutional background. It does not connect any examiner to an LLM, or establish that these deposits were disclosed.

The recurrence

The web recurs. The same six report contributors appear in the same 2024 ex parte meetings. The same vendor whose software carried the boilerplate now sells the LLM integration. The same Library that restricted generative AI in 2024 was drafting the exclusion doctrine in 2025. The same Office that proposed "compilation of AI-generated content" across nine applications was staffed by personnel whose public records place them across the table from OpenAI, Meta, Microsoft, Intel, IBM, Cisco, and HPE — and whose alumni include an OpenAI Senior Copyright Counsel. AtomBeam sits at the far end of routes that pass through HPE, NVIDIA, Intel, and Oracle/DXC, none of which the record connects to this applicant's claims.

The dossier does not establish that these threads touch these files. That is precisely the finding. No internal referral note. No access log. No model-invocation record. No disclosure event. No consultation roster. The absence is not exoneration; it is an evidentiary gap in a system that produced a demonstrable pattern of unaccountable narrowing while its personnel, its software vendor, and its AI-policy apparatus all pointed toward the same private-sector counterparties.

Scope

Correspondence exhibits (E01–E25, S01–S15, P02–P03); application register and observed status; examiner-by-examiner adverse review; legal and procedural authorities; AI-policy personnel review (AP01–AP02); Oracle/Siebel procurement chronology (OP01–OP04); Oracle–AtomBeam relationship limits (OP05); source inventory with SHA-256 verification; consolidated web-source index (141 distinct URLs).

04 / A DEMAND FOR RECORD-BASED ANSWERS

Show the work.
Show the decisions.
Show who was responsible.

  1. Who led and certified the SDA work after Frandeen’s death, and where is the accepted designation?
  2. What happened at the proposed Navy PCM demonstration? Identify the office, criteria, results and safeguards.
  3. Where is the claim-specific explanation for the disputed examination mappings?
  4. Where are the exact original uploads that match the February filing receipts?
  5. What did the applicant authorize the Copyright Office to amend, and what did the final records actually record?
  6. What direct record, if any, connects corporate technology relationships to the handling of these works?

Reputation is not a substitute for the record. An unanswered question does not disappear because the institution has moved on.

05 / INTEGRITY IS PART OF THE CHALLENGE

The evidence must survive scrutiny—including ours.

The record also preserves findings against alteration claims: 14 metadata-only PDF changes, 314 unchanged rendered pages, five unchanged original-file controls, and OCR errors that initially looked like textual differences. Those are corrections, not evidence of tampering.

The Synthetic Children comparisons retain the actual excerpts and feature scores, including MorphicSynth. The scores describe assessed overlap; they do not measure theft. The record has not established a route from these manuscripts into patent drafting, coordinated corruption, a cause of Frandeen’s death, or demonstrated synthetic consciousness. The title states our ethical concern about the direction of these technologies.

Our position is explicit: possible future beings deserve more than a place in a military product strategy. Institutions exercising power over authorship and technology owe precise, inspectable answers.
